The emerging field of sociogenomics has further blurred this distinction, revealing that the genes of one individual can influence the phenotypes of others through indirect genetic effects (IGEs), creating a form of extended heritability that transcends the individual organism. In conservation biology, deviations from HWE in endangered populations serve as early warning indicators of inbreeding and loss of genetic diversity, guiding management interventions such as translocation and captive breeding. In forensic DNA analysis, the Hardy-Weinberg principle underpins the calculation of match probabilities in DNA profiling, where the genotype frequencies of short tandem repeat (STR) markers are estimated from population databases, assuming HWE, to provide statistical evidence in criminal investigations. Finally, the law has been extended to address the challenges of environmental DNA (eDNA) and metagenomic studies, where deviations from HWE can reveal selective pressures in microbial communities and inform our understanding of community dynamics. The Hardy-Weinberg law, therefore, remains a cornerstone of theoretical and applied population genetics, providing a quantitative foundation for understanding the evolutionary and ecological forces shaping genetic diversity in populations."
